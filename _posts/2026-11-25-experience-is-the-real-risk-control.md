Most conversations about a new clinical engagement start with the budget. In my experience, the budget is rarely what decides whether a study succeeds. What decides it is whether the right experienced people are in place at the right time, on both the sponsor and the provider side, and whether the scope they agreed on survives contact with reality.

In my [November overview of the 2026 market](/blog/2026/11/14/clinical-research-2026-selective-recovery/) I described a recovery that was real but selective. This article looks at the practical risks underneath it, as I see them from inside the business: the scarcity of experienced managers in Europe, the cost of postponing decisions, stretched CRO teams, scope that keeps growing, and sponsor teams whose own inexperience is almost never part of the risk assessment.

## 1. Experienced managers are scarce, even in a cooling job market

On paper, 2026 should have made hiring easier in Europe. The EU job vacancy rate [fell to 2.0% in the second quarter](https://www.staffingindustry.com/news/global-daily-news/netherlands-leads-eu-in-job-vacancies-as-overall-rate-falls), and unemployment rose to [6.2% in September 2026](https://www.staffingindustry.com/news/global-daily-news/eu-jobless-rate-rises-to-62-in-september), with 471,000 more people out of work than a year earlier.

Clinical research tells a different story. Clinical hiring in Europe is forecast to [grow by 10.3% in 2026](https://www.cpl.com/uk/insights/life-sciences-industry-analytics-report-clinical), well ahead of the broader scientific labour market, and recruiters single out [clinical project management and clinical operations](https://www.mrlcg.com/resources/blog/life-sciences-recruitment-in-2026--tackling-the-clinical---regulatory-skills-crunch/) as areas where the shortage is most acute. Underneath sits a demographic problem: the EU's working-age population fell from [268 million in 2009 to 260 million in 2025](https://publications.jrc.ec.europa.eu/repository/bitstream/JRC147054/JRC147054_01.pdf) and keeps shrinking by roughly a million people a year, while 79% of EU companies now name a lack of skilled staff as a barrier to investment.

One comparison sums up the European picture. Pharmaceutical R&D investment in Europe rose from [€38.7 billion in 2020 to an estimated €60 billion in 2025](https://www.efpia.eu/media/owqczcqz/the-pharmaceutical-industry-in-figures-2026.pdf), about 55% more. R&D employment grew from roughly 121,000 to an estimated 125,000, about 3%. Far more money is now chasing complex programmes through a workforce that has hardly grown.

The scarcest group is not junior staff. It is mid-level to senior managers who have actually led studies through start-up, recruitment problems, amendments and inspections. Those people are rarely on the open market: an estimated [70% of senior life sciences roles in Europe are never advertised](https://panda.sites.sourceflow.co.uk/insights/candidate-european-pharma-talent-trends-2026-roles-in-demand-and-market-outlook/), and many experienced professionals have moved to contract or consultancy work.

## 2. The cost of waiting: postponed engagements

One trend first became clearly visible in the second and third quarters of 2024 and, with certain fluctuations, is still present today. A sponsor or CRO agrees that it needs senior support, then postpones the engagement because a deal is not yet signed, funding is pending, or a "bigger project" may start soon.

The industry's own numbers trace the same pattern. In mid-2024 Medpace, a CRO focused on small and mid-sized biotechs, [cut its sales guidance after a bookings miss](https://inkl.com/news/ibd-50-s-medpace-takes-a-nosedive-after-unexpectedly-slashing-its-sales-view). By the third quarter, ICON reported that [slower decision-making in biotech had led to award delays and slow trial starts](https://fintool.com/app/research/companies/ICLR/earnings/Q3%202024). Delays and cancellations stayed [severely elevated through much of 2025](https://www.castoredc.com/blog/cro-rebound-2025-ai-disruption-q4-breakdown/) before easing late in the year. Then they rose again: in the first quarter of 2026 Medpace reported [elevated cancellations and a net book-to-bill of 0.88](https://finance.yahoo.com/markets/stocks/articles/medp-q1-deep-dive-rising-050923356.html), while by the second quarter [decision timelines had shortened](https://www.lincolninternational.com/perspectives/articles/pharma-services-deep-dive-the-k-shaped-recovery-is-underway/) as funding returned. The hesitation has eased and returned in waves, but it has not gone away.

Each individual postponement is understandable. Together they are expensive.

While the decision waits, the study does not. Start-up activities drift, feasibility ages, vendors are selected by default and early design choices are made without the person who would have challenged them. By the time the engagement is approved, the experienced professional who was available has often committed elsewhere, and the replacement search starts from zero in the tight market described above.

The arithmetic is unforgiving. Tufts CSDD puts the direct cost of running a Phase II or III trial at [roughly $40,000 per day across phases, and nearly $56,000 per day in Phase III](https://www.appliedclinicaltrialsonline.com/view/economics-clinical-trial-execution-pharma), with delayed market entry worth about $500,000 a day in unrealised sales. Against those numbers, a few weeks of hesitation over a senior engagement is rarely a saving.

## 3. Look beyond the day rate: what clients should really be buying

Sponsors understandably compare offers on price. But when a sponsor engages a senior professional, the real purchase is risk reduction: fewer wrong turns, earlier warnings and decisions that hold up later.

The data show what happens without it. The average number of protocol deviations per Phase III trial [rose from 189 to 296 between 2020 and 2025](https://www.appliedclinicaltrialsonline.com/view/recognizing-addressing-execution-translation-clinical-trials). More than [75% of protocols need at least one substantial amendment](https://www.wcgclinical.com/?p=29632), averaging 3.3 per protocol, and each amendment flows into vendor change orders, which [average about $141,000 in Phase II and $535,000 in Phase III](https://www.bioxconomy.com/finance-and-accounting/ai-helps-biopharma-teams-tame-costly-trial-changes).

Any gap or hesitation at management level shows up directly in timelines, execution quality and, ultimately, the study budget. A twenty-year professional and a two-year professional do not deliver the same hour, and the difference in rate is usually small compared with the cost of one avoidable amendment or one month of delay.

## 4. Resourcing limits at small and mid-size CROs

Small and mid-size CROs are often the best fit for emerging biotechs: closer attention, flexible processes and senior people who stay involved. In 2026, many of them are also running at the edge of their capacity.

Turnover in clinical monitoring roles has stayed [stubbornly high relative to other CRO job families](https://bdo.com/insights/tax/key-findings-from-bdos-2024-clinical-research-organization-global-compensation-turnover-survey), and restructuring across the sector continues. Evotec, for example, has cut around [1,400 roles and now employs about 4,200 people](https://www.pulse.bot/human-resources/news/evotec-slashes-staff-as-turnaround-plan-gathers-pace-5b2dbfaa-871b-4319-bdf3-659f3069ae24). For a smaller CRO, losing one experienced project manager or two senior CRAs can affect several studies at once, and there is rarely a bench to absorb it. The result is the same people stretched across more studies, slower responses and senior oversight that exists on the organisation chart but not in daily practice.

## 5. When demanding clients meet stretched teams

Combine stretched provider teams with a demanding or over-demanding client and the pressure lands in one place: scope. Requests that look small, such as an extra report, another review cycle, additional vendor coordination or a weekly call that becomes daily, gradually push the service out of scope.

This is rarely bad faith on either side. Sponsors under investor pressure want more for the same budget. Providers who want to keep the relationship often absorb the work quietly, and CROs sometimes fail to flag [out-of-scope risk until late](https://www.clinicaltrialsarena.com/?p=2391). Unrecorded extra work stretches already thin teams further, and the conflict surfaces months later as a large change order, missed milestones or the departure of exactly the people the study depended on. Clear scope, early escalation and someone senior enough to say "this is a change" protect both sides.

## 6. The risk nobody puts on the register: the sponsor team itself

Risk assessments routinely cover sites, vendors, recruitment and regulators. They almost never cover the sponsor's own team.

Yet the sponsor profile has changed. Emerging biopharma companies accounted for [68% of all clinical trial starts in 2025](https://www.iqvia.com/nl-nl/blogs/2026/05/therapeutic-areas-driving-clinical-trial-growth), up from 56% in 2019. Many are running their first or second trial with a lean team, sometimes without anyone who has led a global study end to end. As one recent column notes, small sponsors face [limited internal experience and resource constraints that make it hard to absorb inefficiencies](https://www.clinicalleader.com/doc/why-protocol-complexity-is-slowing-first-patient-in-and-how-small-sponsors-can-fix-start-up-inefficiencies-0001).

Inexperience on the sponsor side shows up as slow decisions, unclear ownership, unrealistic expectations of the CRO and difficulty judging which problems are serious. It is rarely recognised, because nobody wants to write it down, and therefore rarely mitigated. Acknowledging it early, and filling the gap with experienced oversight, is one of the most effective risk controls a young sponsor can put in place.

## Minimising these risks in your programme

None of these complications is unusual. Together they explain why so many studies run late and over budget even when the science is sound. The good news is that most of them can be reduced, or avoided altogether, if they are addressed before they compound.

- **Fractional clinical leadership** that can start quickly, so engagements do not stall while deals and funding are finalised
- **Operational rescue and audit readiness** for studies where timelines, scope or quality are already under pressure
- **Clinical strategy and protocol design** that reduces avoidable amendments and change orders from the start
- **An established delivery network** across Central and Southeast Europe, from freelance CRAs to full-scope CRO partners, for when internal resources run out
