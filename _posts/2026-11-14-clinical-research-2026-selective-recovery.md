2026 was the year the clinical research market recovered on paper and stayed difficult in practice. The headline numbers improved: capital returned, deals closed, trial starts ticked up. Yet for many sponsors, CROs and sites, the year did not feel like a recovery. It felt like a sorting.

This article sets out the tendencies I have observed over the year from inside the business. Some are unfinished problems inherited from 2025. Others are new, and together they produced a market more layered than any I have worked in over two decades. My reading is simple: money, work and regulatory attention all came back, but each of them came back selectively, and the experienced people needed to deliver the work became harder to find and to keep.

## What 2025 handed over

We entered 2026 carrying four unresolved problems, none of which a calendar change could fix.

- **A capital drought.** By mid-2025 [almost no new biotech offerings were reaching the public market](https://www.tradeandindustrydev.com/industry/bio-pharmaceuticals/biotech-squeeze-cuts-research-are-reshaping-34990), and layoffs that began in 2023 were still running.
- **A weakened US public research base.** Staff reductions of roughly [3,500 at the FDA and at least 1,200 at the NIH](https://www.clinicalleader.com/doc/how-hhs-budget-cuts-and-vaccine-hesitancy-threaten-clinical-trial-operations-and-what-leaders-can-do-0001), plus terminated grants, left sponsors unsure how predictable the agency would be.
- **Policy noise.** Tariffs, most-favoured-nation pricing and the Inflation Reduction Act all weighed on portfolio decisions at once.
- **Fragile backlogs.** CRO order books looked healthy, but cancellations were, in the words of Medpace's chief executive, [the biggest wild card](https://www.fool.com/earnings/call-transcripts/2025/11/27/medpace-medp-q3-2025-earnings-call-transcript/) for 2026.

The practical effect was a habit of hesitation. Sponsors had learned to delay starts, re-bid work and keep teams thin. That habit outlasted the conditions that created it.

## Funding returned, but not to everyone

The capital markets reopened in 2026, and the money went to late-stage, de-risked assets. J.P. Morgan counts [$23.9 billion of biopharma venture funding across 385 rounds through September](https://www.jpmorgan.com/content/dam/jpmorgan/documents/cb/insights/outlook/jpm-biopharma-deck-q3-2026.pdf), with IPO proceeds already above every annual total from 2022 to 2025. M&A reached $138.9 billion through three quarters, above the $122.1 billion recorded in all of 2025.

The other half of the picture is less comfortable. In the first half of the year total venture dollars rose 22%, while [seed and Series A round counts fell to a decade low](https://www.biocentury.com/article/660172/seed-and-series-a-financing-lagged-biotech-s-broader-recovery-in-1h26) and early-stage capital dropped 15%. GlobalData reached the same conclusion: the [recovery favours later-stage, lower-risk programmes](https://www.globaldata.com/media/pharma/biotech-funding-recovery-favors-late-stage-lower-risk-assets-reveals-globaldata/).

This matters for clinical research because emerging biopharma sponsored [68% of trial starts in 2025](https://www.iqvia.com/blogs/2026/05/therapeutic-areas-driving-clinical-trial-growth). When the smallest companies cannot raise, first-in-human and early Phase II work thins out, and the effect reaches the late-stage pipeline several years later.

## The CRO market: more work, less certainty

Demand for outsourced clinical services improved in 2026, but revenue and confidence lagged the bookings. Fortrea is a fair illustration: a [book-to-bill of 1.12 in the third quarter, revenue still slightly below the prior year](https://www.taiwannews.com.tw/en/news/6468173), and cancellations its management described as [slightly elevated](https://www.thecerbatgem.com/?p=10449680). Biotech bookings grew, yet awarded work took longer to become active studies.

Three sponsor behaviours characterised the year:

1. **Longer decisions.** Vendor selection that once took weeks now runs through several rounds of re-scoping and price comparison.
2. **Smaller commitments.** More sponsors split programmes across functional providers or regional specialists instead of awarding one full-service contract.
3. **Scrutiny of every line.** Pass-through costs, change orders and project management hours are questioned in a way they were not three years ago.

The structure of the supply side added to the complexity. Large CROs kept consolidating, mid-size players merged to gain scale, and investors marked the sector down early in the year on fears that AI would erode billable work, even while [bookings were rising](https://www.castoredc.com/blog/cro-rebound-2025-ai-disruption-q4-breakdown/). A sponsor choosing a partner in 2026 was choosing in a market whose shape was still changing.

## One headline, two markets

The largest players closed 2026 with results their shareholders can be satisfied with; small and mid-size biotechs and CROs describe a different year.

At the top, the numbers speak for themselves. IQVIA reported [third-quarter revenue of $4.43 billion, up 8%, with net new bookings up 15%](https://finviz.com/news/408579/iqvia-reports-third-quarter-2026-results), raised its full-year guidance and saw its [shares rise on the day](https://stockstory.org/us/stocks/nyse/iqv/news/earnings/iqvias-nyseiqv-q3-2026-beats-on-revenue). One market summary called it the [strongest clinical bookings quarter since 2022](https://247wallst.com/cards/iqvia-q3-2026-earnings-iqv-01m5yh1676kcnq1d71870819m2). Large pharma, for its part, used strong balance sheets to buy and license pipelines.

Below that tier the story changes. Analysts at Third Bridge asked openly [why CROs are not fully benefiting from the biotech recovery](https://www.thirdbridge.com/en-us/about-us/media/perspectives/why-cros-miss-biotech-recovery). Consolidation keeps pulling scale toward the biggest providers, and the [undifferentiated middle keeps struggling](https://www.clinicalleader.com/doc/everyone-is-a-consultant-now-0001). In China the squeeze became a price war: one report describes [Phase III oncology bids at less than half the level of three years ago](https://www.huxiu.com/article/4889820.html) and smaller CROs selling themselves to survive.

Small and mid-size biotechs voice the same disconnect. Sector indices and deal totals say the recovery is here. Their own experience says funding is conditional, timelines are compressed, and a large vendor's attention goes first to its largest accounts.

Both views are accurate, because they describe different markets. Shareholder value at the top was built partly on scale, buybacks, offshoring and automation. Those same levers translate, further down the chain, into lower rates, thinner teams and tougher terms. An industry can report record quarters while much of its working base feels poorer, and that is where we stood in 2026.

## More demanding sponsors, fewer experienced people

The sharpest contradiction of 2026 was this: biotech companies asked for more, while the pool of highly qualified, experienced and adequately paid professionals shrank at almost every level.

On the demand side, biotech sponsors now expect senior attention, faster start-up, tighter budgets and near-continuous reporting, often on studies whose protocols are more complex than before. Investor pressure explains much of it. A company funded for one decisive readout cannot tolerate a slow month, and it passes that pressure down the chain to the CRO, the monitor and the site.

On the supply side, experience has been leaving the system. Nearly half of life sciences leaders name [turnover and retention as their main hiring concern](https://www.medixteam.com/?p=43008). At sites, [more than 40% report staffing shortages across all roles](https://resource.advarra.com/hubfs/eBook/Advarra%20Workforce%20Development%20Survey%20Report%20EB.pdf), and the people who remain are asked to run trials with [more procedures and tighter visit windows](https://www.clinicaltrialvanguard.com/news/nih-grant-terminations-expose-structural-crisis-at-clinical-research-sites/).

What I see in practice is a quiet substitution. Senior project managers, experienced CRAs and seasoned medical monitors are replaced by less experienced staff at lower rates, or their roles are stretched across more studies. Budgets are negotiated as if a twenty-year professional and a two-year professional delivered the same hour. Many of the most capable people have responded by leaving permanent roles altogether; one commentator described a workforce [rearranging itself around expertise delivered on demand](https://www.clinicalleader.com/doc/everyone-is-a-consultant-now-0001).

The arithmetic does not work. Higher expectations and thinner experience produce protocol deviations, rework, delayed database locks and inspection findings, which cost far more than the rates that were saved. Experience is the cheapest form of risk mitigation a sponsor can buy, and 2026 showed how often it is treated as the first line to cut.

## When the plan meets reality

A recurring scene in 2026 was sponsor disappointment at the moment a plan had to be revised: timelines, enrolment curves and milestones adjusted to match what was actually happening at execution level.

Revision itself is normal. More than half of trials now run at least 20% behind their original schedule, according to a [Bain survey](https://www.bioxconomy.com/clinical-and-research/recruitment-challenges-delays-and-ai-reshaping-trials-sector-survey-finds). What stood out this year was the reaction. The adjustment was treated as a failure of delivery, with little acknowledgement that certain risks had not been recognised adequately, or in time, when the plan was first set.

The pattern is familiar to anyone who has run studies:

- **Plans built to win approval.** Start-up and enrolment assumptions are set to satisfy a board, an investor or a bid defence, with best-case figures presented as the baseline.
- **Feasibility done thinly or late.** Competing trials, site capacity, regulatory timelines and seasonal effects are discovered during execution instead of before it.
- **Early warnings softened.** Teams under pressure report risks cautiously, and sponsors under pressure hear them selectively.
- **Blame before analysis.** When the gap becomes visible, the first conversation is about who is responsible, and the corrective one is postponed.

The cost is time. Weeks spent on disappointment and escalation are weeks in which nothing changes at the sites.

Programmes recover best when they do three things. They acknowledge openly which risks were missed and why. They re-baseline once, on real data, instead of making repeated small concessions. And they follow with actions that are balanced, applicable and achievable: measures the team can actually implement with the people, budget and countries available, with clear owners and dates. A realistic plan agreed in month six is worth more than an ambitious one defended until month twelve.

## Regulation moved faster than operations could follow

Regulators changed more in 2026 than in any recent year, and most of the change pointed toward flexibility. The difficulty was absorbing it all at once.

| Development | When | What it changes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [FDA single pivotal trial default](https://www.appliedclinicaltrialsonline.com/view/fda-removes-two-study-requirement-new-drug-approval-process) | February 2026; [draft guidance June 2026](https://raps.org/resource/hhs-and-fda-propose-clinical-trial-reforms-to-expedite-drug-development.html) | One adequate, well-controlled trial plus confirmatory evidence as the usual basis for approval |
| [ICH E6(R3) national adoption](https://www.caidya.com/resources/regulatory-updates-on-clinical-trials-december-2025/) | China 31 March, Canada 1 April, UK April 2026 | Risk-proportionate quality management and data governance become inspection expectations |
| [ICH E6(R3) Annex 2](https://intuitionlabs.ai/articles/ich-e6-r3-gcp-guidelines-2025) | Step 4 in June 2026; EU effect 15 January 2027 | Decentralised and pragmatic trial elements receive a harmonised GCP basis |
| [EU Biotech Act proposal](https://www.iqvia.com/en-gb/locations/emea/blogs/2026/06/the-eu-biotech-act-part-2) | In the legislative process | Multinational trial authorisation cut from 106 to 75 days; stronger reporting member state |

Two observations from practice. First, a single pivotal trial lowers cost but raises the price of every design and execution error, because there is no second study to absorb it. Second, E6(R3) is an operating-model change, and a quality system that is an E6(R2) document with a new title page will not meet it.

## Geography became a strategic question again

Where a trial runs was a board-level topic in 2026. China initiated roughly [a third of global Phase I to III trials in the first quarter](https://www.pharmaceutical-technology.com/newsletters/clinical-trials-day-2026-q1-study-trends), and Chinese-origin assets now feed a large share of Western licensing. The US responded with policy aimed explicitly at keeping early trials at home.

Europe spent the year confronting its own numbers. The EEA share of global clinical trials [fell from 18% in 2013 to 9% in 2023](https://accelerating-clinical-trials.europa.eu/system/files/2026-06/Update%20on%20Biotech%20Act%20and%20EU4Health%20Initiative.pdf), and the Biotech Act is the Commission's answer. Encouragingly, study initiations in Europe [grew 9.8% year on year in the first quarter](https://www.clinicaltrialsarena.com/news/clinical-trials-day-2026-q1-study-trends/).

My own view, formed over many years of running global programmes, is that country selection has become too conservative. Sponsors under cost pressure default to the same few markets and then compete with each other for the same sites and patients. Central and Eastern Europe remains underused relative to what it offers: experienced investigators, concentrated patient populations, EU-standard regulation and cost levels that suit a constrained budget. Faster EU authorisation timelines would strengthen that case further.

## AI: widely piloted, rarely embedded

AI in clinical operations was everywhere in conversation and limited in daily use. Bain found [65% of sponsors and CROs piloting AI tools, but only 16% redesigning roles and workflows around them](https://www.bain.com/insights/clinical-trials-face-persistent-challenges-and-new-opportunities/), and most apply the tools in fewer than a quarter of their studies. At sites the gap is wider still: under [9% have adopted AI or technology to ease staffing pressure](https://sitesolutionssummit.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/20260601-0945-Landscape-WEST_5.26.26_V1.pdf).

Meanwhile the underlying operational problems did not improve. The same Bain survey reports that trials delayed by 20% or more [rose from 44% before 2020 to 54% today](https://www.bioxconomy.com/clinical-and-research/recruitment-challenges-delays-and-ai-reshaping-trials-sector-survey-finds), with site capacity tightening as sponsors plan more studies.

The commercial consequence arrived before the operational one. Sponsors now expect AI-driven savings to appear in CRO pricing, whether or not the efficiency has materialised in the delivery team. That expectation gap is a predictable source of friction in contract negotiations.

## What this means for sponsors

For small and mid-size sponsors, 2026 rewarded discipline over ambition. Five lessons stand out.

1. **Design for one decisive trial.** If a single pivotal study may carry the application, invest earlier in endpoints, statistics and feasibility. Savings on design are false economies.
2. **Match the partner to the programme.** A focused study rarely needs a top-five CRO. A regional full-service partner with senior oversight often delivers faster and at lower cost.
3. **Treat country selection as a lever.** Run feasibility across a wider set of countries before defaulting to the usual ones.
4. **Operationalise E6(R3) now.** Risk-based quality management and data governance should be built into the protocol and vendor contracts, with documented rationale.
5. **Ask for evidence on AI.** Request specific examples of where a vendor's tools shortened a timeline, and tie pricing to delivery.

## Looking toward 2027

I expect 2027 to be the year the selective recovery either broadens or hardens into a permanent two-tier market. Three signals will tell us which: whether seed and Series A funding recovers, whether the EU Biotech Act keeps its trial provisions through the legislative process, and whether CRO backlogs convert into started studies at a normal pace.

The fundamentals remain sound. Science is productive, capital is available for good data, and regulators are more open to efficient designs than at any point in my career. The sponsors who will benefit are those who plan with precision and choose their partners and geographies deliberately.
